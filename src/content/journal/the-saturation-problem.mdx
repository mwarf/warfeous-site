There is a version of the AI content story that sounds like good news for marketers.

Faster production. Lower costs. Scalable output. A team of two can now produce what used to require a team of ten. The tools are genuinely impressive, and the efficiency gains are real.

But there is a version of this story that nobody is talking about loudly enough: when everyone has access to the same efficiency gains, the gains cancel out. And what you are left with is a louder room and a shorter attention span.

## The attention economy was already broken

Before generative AI, the content landscape was already oversaturated. The average person encounters somewhere between six and ten thousand brand messages per day. Social media feeds are engineered to maximize time on platform, which means the content competing for any given second of attention is enormous.

Against that backdrop, most branded content was already underperforming. Completion rates on video content were declining. Organic reach on most platforms had been falling for years. The signal-to-noise ratio was already poor.

Generative AI did not create this problem. But it has accelerated it dramatically.

## What happens when content becomes free to produce

When production cost drops toward zero, the rational response for most organizations is to produce more. More posts, more videos, more variations, more channels. And in the short term, some of that volume pays off. More attempts means more chances to land something.

But volume has a ceiling effect. At some point, adding more content to a saturated channel produces diminishing returns, and then negative returns. The audience learns to filter faster. Platform algorithms respond by further restricting organic reach. The cost of breaking through increases even as the cost of production decreases.

The net result is a treadmill. Organizations produce more and more content to maintain the same level of attention, while the cost of that attention keeps rising. It is the opposite of efficiency.

## Where attention is actually going

Here is what the data shows: attention is not disappearing. It is consolidating.

Audiences are spending more time with content they have actively chosen and less time with content served to them algorithmically. Long-form video is growing. Podcast listening is up. Newsletter open rates, for the newsletters that survive the cull, are higher than they have been in years. Readers are finding specific voices they trust and following them across platforms.

What this describes is not an audience that cannot pay attention. It is an audience that has developed better filters. They are very good at deciding, within seconds, whether something is worth their time. And they are increasingly unwilling to give that time to content that feels generic, automated, or produced without genuine regard for them.

The content that is winning in this environment has a specific quality in common. It feels like it came from somewhere. A particular person, with a particular perspective, who made a specific decision about what to include and what to leave out. That editorial judgment is what audiences are responding to, and it is exactly what automated content production cannot replicate.

## The scarcity shift

For most of the past decade, the scarce resource in content was production capability. Good video was expensive. Good writing took time. Organizations that could produce quality content at volume had a real advantage.

That scarcity is gone. Production capability is now abundant and cheap.

What is scarce now is context, perspective, and editorial judgment. The ability to look at a situation and decide what the story actually is. The willingness to take a position rather than optimize for inoffensiveness. The specific, observed detail that makes a piece of content feel like it was made by someone who was paying attention.

These things cannot be generated at scale. They require a human who is genuinely engaged with the subject matter, has developed taste over time, and is willing to make choices that reflect a real point of view.

## The practical implication

For organizations thinking about their content strategy, the saturation problem points toward a counterintuitive conclusion: less, better, and more specific.

One piece of content that is genuinely useful, genuinely observed, and genuinely reflective of a clear point of view will outperform ten pieces of optimised, automated content. Not because of some romantic belief in craft for its own sake, but because that is what the attention data shows.

The organizations winning the content game right now are not the ones producing the most. They are the ones whose audiences trust them enough to seek them out. That trust is built through consistency of voice, specificity of perspective, and content that feels like it came from a real place.

You cannot automate trust. You can only earn it.
