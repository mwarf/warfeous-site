There is a skill that did not exist five years ago and is now quietly essential: the ability to recognize AI-generated writing on sight.

Not because AI tools are always bad. Not because everything they produce is worthless. But because your audience has this skill, they are developing it faster than most organizations realize, and they are using it to make judgments about credibility.

If your brand's content reads like it was generated, that is a trust problem. And the first step to solving it is being able to see what your audience sees.

## What AI writing actually looks like

The patterns have shifted as the models have improved. The early tells, the overuse of words like "delve" and "tapestry," the endless variation of "it is worth noting that," have faded as developers have tuned them out. But the underlying fingerprints remain.

The most reliable signal is a particular kind of structural smoothness. AI-generated content flows from point to point without friction. Every paragraph connects to the next. Every claim is supported. The argument builds cleanly toward a satisfying conclusion. Nothing surprises you.

This sounds like good writing. In many ways it is. But human writing, at its best, has texture that AI cannot replicate. The sentence that sits a little uncomfortably because the writer was not sure how to say something difficult. The specific detail that is almost too specific, clearly drawn from a moment the writer actually experienced. The opinion that goes a little further than the evidence strictly supports, because the writer has seen enough to feel confident about the call.

AI produces the median version of a well-structured argument. Human writing at its best produces something more uneven, more specific, and more alive.

## The vocabulary of generated content

The word patterns shift by era and by model, but a few have proven durable.

Present participial phrases appear two to five times more often in AI-generated writing than in human writing. "The system analyzes the data, revealing key insights." "Drawing on decades of experience, the team delivers results." These constructions are not wrong. They just appear with a frequency that starts to feel mechanical.

The "from X to Y" construction is another one. "From bustling cities to serene landscapes." "From startups to established enterprises." "From strategy to execution." Every few paragraphs, the same template.

Sentences that begin with "This" followed by a summary of the previous point. "This approach allows organizations to..." "This shift reflects a broader trend..." The AI is confirming that it tracked what it just said before it moves on. Human writers do this occasionally. AI does it constantly.

And the paragraph that ends with a slightly vague but optimistic forward-looking statement. "As the landscape continues to evolve, the organizations that adapt will be positioned to thrive." It sounds like a conclusion. It does not actually say anything.

## Why your audience notices before you do

The people reading your content have developed detection skills through exposure, not through deliberate study. They cannot always articulate what feels off. But they know.

A 2025 study found that people who use AI tools regularly correctly identify AI-generated text about ninety percent of the time. People who rarely use AI tools do only slightly better than random chance at identifying it, but they still experience the content as somehow less engaging, less trustworthy, and less worth their time.

"Hollow" is the word that keeps appearing in audience research. People feel the absence of a specific human intelligence behind the text, even when they cannot explain what that absence consists of.

For brands, this is significant. Content that reads as hollow is not neutral. It actively erodes the trust you are trying to build. It tells the audience, at some level below conscious articulation, that nobody who matters thought carefully about what they were saying to them.

## The QA pass

If you are using AI tools in your content production, the most important thing you can do is build a human review step that is specifically looking for the patterns described above.

Not just a proofread. Not a spell check. A deliberate pass asking: does this sound like a specific person with a real point of view, or does it sound like a well-organized average?

The questions to ask: Is there anything here that could only have been written by someone who actually knows this subject from the inside? Is there a sentence that takes a genuine position rather than presenting multiple perspectives and letting the reader decide? Is there a specific detail that feels observed rather than generated?

If the answer to all three is no, the content needs more human input before it goes out.

## What good looks like

The goal is not to sound unpolished. It is to sound like a specific person who knows what they are talking about.

The best AI-assisted content uses the tools for what they are good at, which is structure, drafting, and iteration, and reserves the human judgment for what matters most: the specific claims, the genuine opinions, the observed details, and the voice that holds everything together.

The audience cannot tell how you made the content. They can tell whether a human who cared about the subject was genuinely involved in making it.

That involvement is what they are looking for. And right now, it is increasingly what sets credible brand content apart from everything else competing for the same attention.
