Every region competing for investment has essentially the same toolkit.

Shovel-ready land. Tax incentives. Workforce development programs. Competitive operating costs. A quality of life pitch built around affordability, proximity to nature, and a growing population. These are real advantages, and every economic development organization in the country leads with them.

The problem is that every economic development organization in the country leads with them.

When every region is offering roughly comparable incentives and roughly comparable amenities, the differentiating factor is no longer the package. It is the confidence an investor or site selector feels about the place. Whether it feels real. Whether they trust the people they would be working with. Whether they can imagine their business actually thriving there.

That is a trust problem. And trust is not solved with a policy document. It is built through stories.

## How site selectors actually make decisions

If you have ever talked at length with a commercial real estate broker or a corporate site selector, the thing that stands out is how much of their process is qualitative.

Yes, they run the numbers. Labour costs, logistics, utility rates, tax burden, incentive structures. The spreadsheet has to work. But the spreadsheet alone has never closed a deal. What closes deals is when the numbers work and the place feels right.

Feeling right is hard to define and easy to underestimate. It includes things like: do the local economic development staff seem to actually know the community, or are they reciting a brochure? Does the region have companies in similar industries that have succeeded there, and are those companies willing to talk about it? Is there evidence of momentum, of things being built and people showing up, rather than just potential waiting to be unlocked?

These are perception questions. And perception is shaped almost entirely by what a region chooses to put in front of people.

## The credibility gap

Most economic development marketing is built on aspiration. Here is what we could be. Here is our potential. Here is the vision we are working toward.

Aspiration is not wrong. But aspiration without evidence reads as wishful thinking. And investors, who are professional risk evaluators, are very good at sensing when a pitch is heavier on hope than on proof.

The credibility gap opens between what a region claims and what it can demonstrate. Closing that gap requires something economic development organizations are sometimes reluctant to do: let real people tell real stories, including the complicated ones.

A founder who moved their operation to your region and found it harder than expected, but stayed and grew, is more credible than a polished testimonial from someone who only has good things to say. A company that ran into regulatory challenges and worked through them with local staff is more compelling than a list of available incentives. The imperfect story, told honestly, builds more trust than the perfect pitch.

This is counterintuitive for organizations that have spent years polishing their brand. But it is consistent with how trust actually works between people, and between people and places.

## What the video brief should actually say

Investment attraction content is often briefed as a showcase. Show the assets, feature the amenities, present the success stories. The underlying logic is that the more positive evidence you put in front of someone, the more likely they are to feel confident about the region.

But confidence does not come from a quantity of positive data points. It comes from a sense that what you are being shown is true.

The most effective investment attraction content I have worked on starts from a different brief: what would a skeptical, intelligent person need to see and hear to genuinely trust this place? What are the real questions they have, and who is best positioned to answer them credibly?

Those questions lead you away from polished showcases and toward specific, human stories. A manufacturer who can explain in plain terms why they chose this region over four alternatives. A business owner who has been operating there for fifteen years and has a considered opinion about what works and what does not. A worker who can describe the labour market from the inside.

These are not glamorous stories. They do not require drone footage or a sweeping orchestral score. They require finding the right people, earning their trust, and capturing something genuine on camera.

## The compounding effect

Trust, once built, compounds. A region that has consistently shown up with honest, specific, credible content builds a reputation that precedes every pitch meeting. The economic development staff walk into conversations with a tailwind instead of starting from zero.

The opposite is also true. A region that consistently leads with overpromised aspiration and generic marketing builds a reputation for that too. And that reputation is very hard to overcome when a serious investor starts doing their own due diligence.

The regions winning the investment attraction game right now are not the ones with the best incentive packages. They are the ones that have figured out how to make serious people trust them. That trust is built in the same way it is built between individuals: through honest, specific, consistent communication over time.

A well-told true story is the most efficient investment attraction tool available. It works while you sleep, it gets shared by the people who find it useful, and it builds the kind of credibility that no press release can manufacture.

The package matters. But trust closes the deal.
