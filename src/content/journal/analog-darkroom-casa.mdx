I spend most of my professional life working fast.

Fast turnarounds. Fast edits. Fast decisions about what to keep and what to cut. My whole career is built around compressing the gap between raw material and finished story, and lately I have been building tools specifically designed to do that faster.

So it surprised me a little, a few weeks ago, to find myself standing in the darkroom at CASA on a Wednesday evening, completely still, watching an image slowly appear in a tray of developer. Waiting. Not checking my phone. Not running a mental to-do list. Just watching.

I had signed up for the [Film Photography class at CASA Lethbridge](https://casalethbridge.ca/classes/film-photography-section-1/) with instructor Kort W. on something between an instinct and a hunch. Wednesday nights, 7 to 9. Black and white. Manual settings, film development, darkroom printing with an enlarger. The whole process.

What I got was something I did not know I needed: a full stop.

## The camera in the bag

I am not shooting on a borrowed camera from the class kit. I am shooting on my dad's Yashicamat 124G. A medium format twin-lens reflex that takes 120 film and produces these big, square, beautiful negatives. My dad had it for years. He passed away a few years ago, and the camera came to me along with a few other things.

I had not picked it up in a while. Life and work and the pace of digital production had made it easy to leave it in the bag.

Coming back to it in this class has been something I did not expect. There is a particular quality to holding a camera that someone who mattered to you also held and looked through. You frame the same square viewfinder they framed. Your hands fall into the same positions. The shutter has the same sound it always had.

It is not a sad experience. It is something quieter than that. Present, in a way that is hard to manufacture.

## The thing about film

The Yashicamat gives me twelve frames per roll of 120 film. Twelve. Not three hundred. Not unlimited. Twelve chances to get it right before I have to think about what I am doing and whether it is worth another roll.

Unlike digital photography, where you can take hundreds of shots in seconds, film gives you a set number of exposures per roll. And this limitation is exactly what many people find compelling. Shooting on film forces you to slow down.

That is not a marketing line. It is a real cognitive shift. When each frame has a cost attached to it, you stop and look. Actually look. You consider the light, the composition, what you are trying to say. You make a decision and then you commit to it, because you cannot take it back.

I have been a visual storyteller for more than 27 years. I know how to look at things. But I had forgotten how to look slowly. Kort's class, and this camera, are reminding me there is a difference between scanning for usable moments and actually seeing what is in front of you.

## What the darkroom does

The printmaking side of the class hit differently.

Darkroom printing is a heady mix of technical precision, creative intuition, and psychological fortitude. You are managing chemistry, light, time, and paper all at once, with no undo button and no preview. You make a test strip. Evaluate it. Adjust the exposure, adjust the contrast, try again. Creating a finished print can take hours: exposing test strips, dodging and burning, refining the balance, and reprinting until every tone feels right.

The class covers aperture and shutter speed, how to match them to the ISO of your film, film development, and making darkroom prints with an enlarger. Composition, subject matter, and contrast are all explored. But what Kort is really teaching, underneath all of that, is patience. Attention. The understanding that the process is not separate from the result. It is the result.

This is not efficient. It is not supposed to be.

What it is, is present. You cannot be distracted in a darkroom. The process will not allow it. And something happens to your thinking when you remove the option of distraction: the work starts to feel like it matters in a way that is hard to manufacture in front of a screen.

## The hollow feeling I had been ignoring

I want to be careful here, because I am not anti-AI. I build AI tools for my own practice. I wrote and spoke about this publicly at the High Level Innovation Conference just last month. I believe AI can be a genuine multiplier for creative work when it is used to handle friction rather than replace judgment.

But I had been noticing something in my own work that I did not have language for until the darkroom gave it to me.

AI can create stunning visuals, but it often feels impersonal and detached from the human experience. I had been feeling that detachment from the inside. Prompting. Generating. Selecting. Iterating. Fast. Efficient. And somehow unsatisfying in a way that was hard to pinpoint.

The research gives it a name. The value of something lies not only in the outcome but also in the effort invested in the process. Objects are accorded greater value when substantial effort has been invested in their creation. The rapid response speed of AI can induce the perception that little effort has been invested in the creative process.

That is true for the audience looking at the work. But I think it is also true for the person making it. When the effort disappears from your own creative process, something else disappears with it. Something that feels like authorship.

The Wednesday nights helped me figure out what I had been missing.

## What slow art gives back

The constraints of analog, the limited exposures, the delayed gratification, necessitate a more considered approach and a heightened attention to the environment and the process itself. This slow photography movement can be viewed as an intentional counter-practice to the pervasive culture of speed and digital fatigue, offering a respite from information overload and constant connectivity.

That is the academic language for what I experienced as just: relief.

Relief from the loop of generate, evaluate, discard, generate again. Relief from the distance between intention and output that AI introduces, where you describe what you want rather than make it with your hands. Relief from the particular exhaustion of creative work that moves too fast to feel like anything.

When you choose to shoot film, you are making a quiet but powerful statement: this moment matters enough to slow down, to risk mistakes, to wait.

I had not been saying that to myself about my work in a while.

And there is something else. When I look through the waist-level viewfinder of the Yashicamat, I am not just slowing down. I am holding something my dad held. I am seeing the world through an instrument he cared about. That is not a workflow optimization. That is something much older than optimization. It is continuity. It is a conversation with someone who is no longer here, carried on in light and silver and paper.

That does not show up in any productivity metric. It does not need to.

## The part that surprised me

I expected to come away from this feeling like analog and digital were opposites, and that I was choosing a side. That is not what happened.

What I came away with was a clearer sense of what each mode of working is actually for. Fast, AI-assisted production has a genuine place in my workflow. It handles the mechanical parts so I can focus on the parts that require judgment. But judgment requires that you have cultivated taste. And taste requires that you have spent time doing slow, intentional, effortful creative work where the outcome is uncertain and the process is the point.

The darkroom is where I go to remember what I am actually trying to do when I pick up a camera. Not produce content. Not hit a deadline. See something. Commit to it. Make it physical.

The revival of analog is not about rejecting technology. It reflects a growing desire for authenticity, presence, and tactile experience in creative expression. As digital fatigue sets in and attention spans fragment under endless content streams, the return to film is not a gimmick but a philosophy of seeing.

That is the part I needed. Not a rejection of the tools I use professionally. A philosophy of seeing to put underneath all of them.

If you are in Lethbridge and you have ever thought about trying this, the [Film Photography class at CASA](https://casalethbridge.ca/classes/film-photography-section-1/) is exactly what you are looking for. Kort is a thoughtful instructor and the darkroom access alone is worth it. Wednesday nights. The building smells like fixer and possibility.

And if you have a camera from someone you loved sitting in a bag somewhere, go get it. Load it. Take twelve frames.

They are still in there with you when you do.
